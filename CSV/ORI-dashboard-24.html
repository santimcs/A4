Method & rationale — how to read this (collapsible)

Click to expand the background notes

Why this section exists. The dashboard already answers “when a stock drops, how often does it bounce?” This card answers the mirror question: “when a stock spikes, how often does it give back the spike so I can buy back lower?” You already own the shares, so a spike is a chance to sell high and re-enter low without changing your long-term share count. The profit is the spread, not a new position.

The strategy in one line. Sell N shares on a ≥5% up-day at the close. Place a buy-back limit at −5% below that price. If it fills within the horizon, your share count returns to N and you keep the spread. If it doesn't fill, you either wait longer or buy back at market — either way you still own the shares, you just haven't banked the spread.

Why we measure the spread, not the “profit”. Unlike the drop→bounce card, here your “entry” is a sell. The relevant return is (sell − buy_back) / sell, which is what you'd actually pocket per share. Because the buy-back target is exactly −5%, every hit shows ≥5% spread; the interesting part is the gap-down tail (bigger spreads) and the Max run-up before hit column (how much the price ran against you before giving back).

Why the 200-MA gate matters here too. Selling a spike below the 200-MA in a falling stock is riskier than selling a spike above the MA in a rising stock: the latter is a normal pullback, the former might be the start of a new leg down (good for the spread) or a dead-cat bounce in a bear trend (bad — you might not get a chance to buy back before the stock recovers past your sell price). The tile “200-MA gate: 100% vs 58%” shows which regime is favourable for your symbol.

Why we do not recommend “concentrate”. Hit rates in this sample are 52%–65%. The wins are capped near 5% spread, and the misses (especially when the price keeps rising) leave you out of N shares at a higher price than you sold — which is a real cost if you're forced to buy back higher. Position size should reflect that asymmetry, not the nominal spread.

Data caveats. (1) The exported 3-yrs-sales-data.csv contains duplicate rows; harmless for base-rate counts but dedupe on date before trusting absolute numbers. (2) Thai holiday gaps compress calendar-day counts; the table reports both Rows and Cal days. (3) The 200-MA is rolling(200) with min_periods=200, so early up-days show below even when the trend looked up.

What to fine-tune next. Change RISE_PCT, TARGET_PCT, HORIZONS_ROWS, or MA_GATE_WINDOW. Suggested experiments: (a) tighten to RISE_PCT=0.06 — fewer but sharper spikes; (b) loosen TARGET_PCT=0.03 — buy-back rate jumps, smaller spread; (c) set MA_GATE_WINDOW=50 for a faster trend filter. Each run overwrites ORI-sim-notes-short.json.
